The patient I want to describe was fifty-eight years old, and she had been told four
times that nothing was wrong with her.

She came to our clinic with fatigue, joint pain, and a rash across her cheeks that
came and went. Her bloodwork was unremarkable. Her imaging was clean. Three
physicians before me had documented, politely, that her symptoms were probably
stress-related. One had suggested she try sleeping more.

Here is what none of us knew at the time. Autoimmune disease in women is diagnosed on
average four and a half years later than the same disease in men presenting with the
same complaints. Four and a half years. That is not a rounding error in a spreadsheet.
That is a decade of a person's life spent being disbelieved.

I want to be careful here, because the easy story is that doctors are dismissive, and
that story is both partly true and mostly useless. The harder story is structural.
Diagnostic criteria for dozens of conditions were derived from cohorts that were
overwhelmingly male. The reference ranges printed on your lab report — the numbers
that decide whether a result is flagged abnormal — were often established decades ago
from populations that looked nothing like the patient in front of you. When a woman's
inflammatory markers sit at the upper edge of a range calibrated on men, the machine
prints "normal" and the clinician moves on.

Her diagnosis, when it finally came, was lupus. It came because a rheumatology fellow
ordered an antibody panel that nobody had thought to order, for the entirely
unscientific reason that her grandmother had died of something similar and the fellow
happened to ask.

So what do we do with that? Three things, and none of them are glamorous.

First, we fix the reference data. This is tedious, expensive, unphotogenic work.
It involves recruiting balanced cohorts and recalculating thresholds that have been
sitting in laboratory software untouched since before I was born. Several countries
have begun. Most have not.

Second, we change what we measure. Chronic pain, fatigue, and cognitive fog are
notoriously difficult to quantify, so we have historically treated them as background
noise rather than data. But a symptom you cannot measure is not a symptom that is
absent. It is a measurement problem, and measurement problems are engineering
problems, and engineering problems are solvable.

Third — and this is the one I keep failing at — we have to build the habit of saying
"I don't know yet" instead of "there's nothing wrong." Those two sentences sound
similar in a seven-minute consultation. They are opposites. One keeps the
investigation open. The other closes a file on a human being.

My patient is doing well now, on a treatment regimen that has been available since
the nineteen-fifties. Nothing exotic was required. What was required was somebody
asking a question that the previous four of us had not thought to ask, and that
includes me.
