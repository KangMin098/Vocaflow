Every quarter for nine years I watched a company destroy value on purpose, and
everyone involved was behaving rationally.

The company made industrial pumps. Good ones. Their margins were healthy, their
customers were loyal, and their engineers had designed a replacement product that
was cheaper to manufacture and lasted twice as long. Management killed it. Twice.

You already know the shape of this story, but I want to argue that the usual moral is
wrong. The usual moral is that incumbents are complacent, that executives are
short-sighted, that big organisations lose their nerve. I have sat in those meetings.
Nobody in them was complacent. They were making a defensible calculation with the
incentives they had been given.

Consider what the replacement product actually did to the balance sheet. It cannibalised
a profitable line. It reduced the aftermarket revenue from spare parts, which was
roughly forty percent of divisional profit and nearly all of the division's stability.
It required retooling two plants, which meant capital expenditure this year against
returns that would arrive, at the earliest, in year four. And the executive
compensation structure paid out on a three-year rolling average of return on capital
employed.

Read that last sentence again. The people deciding were paid on a metric that the
correct decision would depress for exactly as long as they would be measured on it.
This is not a character flaw. This is an accounting choice, made years earlier, by
people who were not thinking about pumps.

Here is what I think we get wrong about disruption. We talk about it as a failure of
foresight, when it is much more often a failure of accounting. Firms are extremely
good at optimising what they measure. The problem is that the standard toolkit —
depreciation schedules, divisional profit and loss, return on invested capital —
was designed for an industrial economy where assets were tangible and product cycles
were long. Applied to a business where the durable asset is an installed base or a
learning curve or a developer ecosystem, the same toolkit systematically undervalues
the things that determine survival.

So the entrant wins. Not because they are smarter, but because they have no
aftermarket revenue to protect, no plants to retool, and no compensation committee
watching a three-year average.

What can an incumbent actually do? I have seen exactly one intervention work
repeatedly, and it is unsatisfying. You have to move the threatening product into a
separate entity with its own profit and loss, its own hiring authority, and its own
metrics — and then you have to protect it from the parent company's finance function
with something close to violence. Every time I have seen this fail, it failed because
somebody sensible said, "let's capture synergies," and the synergies turned out to be
the old incentive structure arriving through the back door.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that strategy is mostly downstream of measurement.
Tell me how a company's leaders are paid and what appears on their monthly report, and
I will predict their strategic decisions more accurately than I could from reading
their strategy.
