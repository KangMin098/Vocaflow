Chris Bennett: You've spent a decade arguing that we measure poverty wrong. Start
there — what are we actually getting wrong?

Dr. Amara Osei: We measure it as a line. You're above it or below it, and the line is
usually some fraction of median income. It's clean, it's comparable across countries,
and it describes almost nothing about what being poor is actually like.

Chris Bennett: Because?

Dr. Amara Osei: Because poverty isn't a level, it's a volatility problem. Take two
households with identical annual incomes. One earns the same amount every month. The
other earns triple in three months and nothing in four. On every official statistic
they're the same household. In lived experience they are not remotely the same
household, and the second one will make decisions that look irrational from the
outside and are entirely rational from the inside.

Chris Bennett: Give me one of those decisions.

Dr. Amara Osei: Buying a smaller quantity at a higher unit price. Everyone's seen the
statistic that poor households pay more per kilogram for basic goods, and the usual
interpretation is a lack of financial literacy. It isn't. If your income arrives
unpredictably, buying in bulk means converting liquid cash into inventory at exactly
the moment when liquidity is the thing protecting you from catastrophe. Paying the
unit-price penalty is the cost of staying solvent. It's insurance.

Chris Bennett: So the advice to plan ahead —

Dr. Amara Osei: — is advice that assumes a predictable future, given to people whose
defining problem is that they do not have one. Yes.

Chris Bennett: What would you measure instead?

Dr. Amara Osei: Income variance alongside income level. Both numbers, always. And a
second thing, which is harder: the size of the shock a household can absorb without
a cascade. Because what destroys people is rarely the poverty itself. It's the car
repair that costs the job, or the missed rent that costs the tenancy reference, or
the medical bill that starts the borrowing.

Chris Bennett: You've called that cascade the real mechanism. Is it measurable?

Dr. Amara Osei: Partially. There's good administrative data in a handful of countries
where you can follow individuals across systems and watch a single event propagate.
It's sobering. In one dataset roughly forty percent of homelessness spells traced back
to a single unanticipated expense under a thousand units of local currency.

Chris Bennett: Under a thousand.

Dr. Amara Osei: Under a thousand. Which tells you something quite hopeful, actually,
because a shock that small is a shock a policy can absorb. Emergency grant schemes
that pay out within seventy-two hours have some of the strongest cost-benefit ratios
in the entire social policy literature, and almost nobody runs them at scale.

Chris Bennett: Why not?

Dr. Amara Osei: Because they're administratively ugly. They require discretion, they
have a visible error rate, and the errors are legible in a way the successes aren't.
If you help nine hundred families and wrongly pay fifty, the fifty are the story.

Chris Bennett: Last question. What changed your mind recently?

Dr. Amara Osei: I used to think the barrier was evidence. It isn't. The evidence has
been sufficient for about fifteen years. The barrier is that our institutions are
optimised to avoid visible mistakes rather than to produce invisible benefits, and I
don't have a research paper that fixes that.
