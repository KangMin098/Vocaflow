My grandmother spoke four languages and was ashamed of three of them.

She was born in a border region that changed hands twice in her lifetime, and each
time it changed hands the school system changed with it. She learned to read in one
language, was punished for speaking it at eleven, was educated in a second, married
into a third, and emigrated into a fourth. By the time I knew her she spoke all of
them with an accent, and she had concluded from this that she spoke none of them
properly.

I study language shift, which is the polite academic term for how communities stop
speaking one language and start speaking another. And the thing I want to say is that
this process is almost never a decision. Nobody convenes a meeting. There is no vote.
It happens through thousands of small calculations made by parents who want their
children to have an easier time than they did.

The pattern is remarkably consistent across continents. The first generation speaks
the heritage language at home and the dominant language badly outside it. The second
generation is fluent in both but answers their parents in the dominant language. The
third generation understands the heritage language and cannot speak it. The fourth
generation has a few words for food and a strong feeling of loss they cannot quite
locate.

Three generations. That is the typical span from full transmission to silence. It is
faster than most people expect and slower than any single family can perceive.

What accelerates it is not hostility, though hostility certainly does the job. What
accelerates it most reliably is prestige asymmetry — the accumulated sense that one
language belongs to the future and the other to the past. When a language is absent
from the workplace, the broadcast, the textbook, and the shop sign, no policy is
required to end it. Parents draw the obvious conclusion and act lovingly on it.

Now, the revitalisation literature is full of failures, and I want to be honest about
that. You cannot legislate a language back into a kitchen. Immersion schools produce
speakers who are fluent in the classroom register and struggle at a family dinner.
Digital archives preserve recordings and preserve nothing else, because a language is
not a set of recordings — it is an economy of situations in which speaking it is the
natural thing to do.

The programmes that have worked share one feature, and it is not the one I expected.
They created contexts where the heritage language was the only practical option for
something people genuinely wanted to do. A radio station people actually listened to.
A workplace where it was the language of business. In one case, a very successful
one, a children's television programme that was simply better than the alternatives.

Prestige, in other words, is not restored by being told to respect something. It is
restored by that something being useful and enjoyable and present.

My grandmother died believing she had no proper language. She was wrong, and the
wrongness was not hers. She had been taught, four separate times, that the way she
spoke was the wrong way, and she believed the people teaching her, because they were
the ones with the schools and the newspapers and the jobs.
