So I spent four years as a professional apologist. That is not the job title. The job
title was Customer Experience Specialist. [Laughter] But what I did, eight hours a
day, was apologise to strangers for things I had not done.

And I got extraordinarily good at it. I want to be clear that this is not a
transferable skill anyone respects. You cannot put "world-class at absorbing rage
about a delayed sofa" on a résumé. [Laughter] I have tried. Twice.

But I did learn something, and I have been trying for years to work out how to say it
without sounding like a poster in a dentist's waiting room.

Here it is. Almost nobody who is shouting is shouting about the thing they are
shouting about.

The sofa is late. Fine. The sofa being late is genuinely annoying, and it deserves
about ninety seconds of annoyance. What I was receiving was forty minutes. And
underneath the forty minutes, reliably, was something else — a house move that had
gone badly, a parent moving in, a divorce, a first proper home after years of
somebody else's furniture. The sofa was carrying a load the sofa had not agreed to
carry. [Laughter]

I used to think my job was to solve the sofa. It is not. There is a script for the
sofa and it takes ninety seconds. My job was the other thirty-eight and a half
minutes, and the technique — this is the whole technique, I am giving away the entire
trade secret — is to stop talking.

Not the sympathetic murmuring. That makes it worse, because sympathetic murmuring is
audibly a technique and everyone can hear it. [Laughter] I mean actual silence.
Someone shouts, they run out of shouting, and there is a pause, and every instinct in
your body says fill the pause. Do not fill the pause. Nine times in ten, they fill it
themselves, and what they put in it is the real thing.

"Sorry. It's been a rough month."

That sentence is the whole job. Everything before it was weather.

Now, I want to be responsible here, because I am aware this sounds like I am
recommending you let people yell at you, and I am not. [Applause] Thank you — that
was three people, I could hear all three of you. [Laughter] There is a difference
between absorbing a feeling and accepting abuse, and the difference is that one of
them stops when you acknowledge it and the other one escalates. I learned to tell
them apart in about eleven months, which is roughly ten months longer than my
employer had budgeted for.

I left that job. I do not miss it. But I notice that I am now the person in my family
who gets phoned when something has gone wrong, and I notice that I no longer rush to
fix it, and I notice that people finish their sentences around me in a way they did
not before.

Which is either a valuable life lesson, or four years of my twenties with a very good
story attached. [Laughter] I genuinely cannot tell, and at this point I have stopped
trying to work it out. Thank you. [Applause]
