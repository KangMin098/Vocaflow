I taught secondary mathematics for eleven years, and for the first seven of them I was
teaching a subject that does not exist.

What I mean is this. The thing I was teaching — the sequence of procedures, the
worked examples, the exercises graded from easy to hard — bore almost no relationship
to what mathematicians do, and more importantly it bore no relationship to how humans
acquire durable understanding. I was very good at it. My students passed. And most of
them could not, eighteen months later, do any of it.

The moment this became undeniable was a retention test I ran, initially out of
curiosity, on a topic I had taught particularly well. Quadratic factorisation. My
class had scored an average of eighty-one percent on the end-of-unit assessment. I
gave them the identical paper, unannounced, seven months later. The average was
thirty-four.

Now, some forgetting is normal and expected. But thirty-four percent is not
forgetting. Thirty-four percent is evidence that whatever they had built was never
load-bearing in the first place.

So I started reading, and what I found was a research literature that has been
remarkably stable for about fifty years and remarkably ignored for about the same
period.

Three findings changed my practice. The first is that retrieval beats review.
Rereading notes feels productive and produces almost nothing; being asked to recall
something without the notes feels uncomfortable and produces durable memory. The
discomfort is not a side effect. It appears to be the mechanism.

The second is spacing. The same total study time distributed across weeks
outperforms the same time massed into one session, often by a factor of two on
delayed tests. Every student knows cramming works. Every student is right — it works
for the exam on Friday and it is nearly worthless by the following month.

The third is interleaving, and this one I resisted hardest. Practice sets in
textbooks are blocked: twenty problems of one type, then twenty of another. It feels
orderly. It produces students who can execute a procedure but cannot recognise which
procedure a novel problem requires — because in a blocked set, the recognition step
has been done for them by the chapter heading. Mixing problem types tanks performance
during practice and improves it substantially on later tests.

Notice the pattern in all three. Every intervention that works makes the lesson feel
worse while it is happening. Students rate interleaved practice as less effective
than blocked practice, while performing better on it. They rate rereading as more
effective than self-testing, while performing worse. Their judgement of their own
learning is not merely imperfect; it is systematically inverted.

That is the hard part of this job. Not the content. Not the classroom management. The
hard part is asking a room of sixteen-year-olds to trust that the approach that feels
harder and produces worse marks this week will produce better understanding in
March — and doing it without any evidence they can perceive in the moment.

I do not have a clean solution to that. What I have is the retention test. I run it
every year now, and I show them the numbers from the previous cohort. It is the only
argument I have found that survives contact with a teenager.
