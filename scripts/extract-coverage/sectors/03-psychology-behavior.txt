I run experiments on procrastination, which means I am professionally obligated to
find this funny, and personally obligated to admit that I submitted the grant
proposal for this research eleven minutes before the deadline.

Here is the finding that reorganised how I think about it. Procrastination is not a
time-management problem. People who procrastinate are not confused about how many
hours are in a day. They are not bad at estimating durations — or rather, they are,
but so is everyone, and that turns out not to predict anything.

What predicts procrastination is mood repair.

Let me unpack that. When you face a task that generates an unpleasant feeling —
anxiety, boredom, self-doubt, resentment, the specific dread of being evaluated —
your nervous system does what nervous systems do, which is look for the fastest route
away from the unpleasant feeling. Cleaning the kitchen is a route. Reorganising your
files is a route. Reading about procrastination research is, I regret to say, an
excellent route.

The relief is immediate. The cost is deferred. And because the cost is deferred, the
behaviour gets reinforced. You are not being lazy. You are being conditioned.

This reframing has a consequence that I find genuinely useful. If the mechanism is
emotional regulation rather than scheduling, then scheduling interventions should not
work very well. And they don't. We have decades of studies on planners, timers,
priority matrices, and app-based blockers, and the effect sizes are small and they
decay fast. People buy the planner. People stop using the planner. This is not a
failure of willpower; it is a treatment aimed at the wrong organ.

What does move the needle is stranger and smaller. Self-forgiveness, for one.
Students who were guided to forgive themselves for procrastinating on a first exam
procrastinated measurably less on the second. That sounds like a greeting card, and I
was sceptical, and the effect replicated. The mechanism appears to be that shame
itself becomes another unpleasant feeling to escape from, so the guilt about
procrastinating fuels more procrastination. Remove the shame and you remove fuel.

Implementation intentions also help — not "I will work on the report," but "when I
sit down at my desk on Tuesday morning, I will open the report and write the first
paragraph." The specificity matters because it removes the moment of decision, and
the moment of decision is where the aversion lives.

And then there is the tactic I am most fond of, which is to make the first action
absurdly small. Not "write the chapter." Open the document. That is the entire task.
You are allowed to close it immediately. Most people don't, because the aversion was
attached to the anticipation, not the activity, and anticipation cannot survive
contact with the actual work.

I want to end on the part that is not tidy. None of this cures anything. I still
submit things late. What changed is that I stopped treating it as a defect of
character and started treating it as a predictable response to a feeling I had not
learned to sit with. That is a smaller claim than a solution. It is also, I think,
a truer one.
