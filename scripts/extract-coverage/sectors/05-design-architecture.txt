The most successful building I ever worked on is one that nobody photographs.

It is a public library in a mid-sized town, four storeys, brick and glass, entirely
unremarkable from the street. No architectural magazine has published it. It has won
nothing. And on a Tuesday afternoon in February there are more people inside it than
in any other building in that town, including the shopping centre.

I want to talk about why, because I think our profession has an accounting problem
similar to the one economists have. We measure the wrong thing, beautifully.

Architecture is evaluated through photographs. That sounds trivial. It is not. A
photograph is taken at a particular hour, in particular weather, usually before anyone
has moved in, and always from a position a human being would not naturally occupy.
Buildings that photograph well are buildings with clean sightlines, dramatic voids,
and no clutter. Buildings that live well are buildings with soft corners, places to
put your coat down, and enough acoustic absorption that a child crying does not
colonise the entire floor plate.

These two categories overlap less than you would hope.

In the library, the decisions that made the difference were almost all invisible. The
entrance has a vestibule deep enough that the winter wind does not reach the reading
area, which sounds like nothing until you compare occupancy rates in January. The
seating is a mixture — hard chairs at tables for people working, upholstered chairs
near windows for people reading, and low benches near the children's section for
parents who need to be within arm's reach but are exhausted. That mixture came from
watching people in the old building for three weeks and writing down what they
actually did rather than what the brief said they would do.

The ceilings are lower than my instincts wanted. I fought this. The acoustician was
right and I was wrong: a high ceiling in a reading room is magnificent for eleven
seconds and then it is a reverberation chamber, and people leave without knowing why
they left.

And the thing I am proudest of is a corridor that is too wide. It is roughly a metre
wider than any circulation standard requires, which cost real money and gained no
lettable area. But it means two people can stop and talk without blocking anyone, and
so people do stop and talk, and a library where people talk to each other in the
corridor is a different institution from one where they do not.

None of this is visible in an elevation drawing. None of it appears in a render. It
comes from observation, from post-occupancy evaluation, from the profoundly
unfashionable discipline of going back to a building three years later and asking what
went wrong.

We do not do that. We hand over, we photograph, we publish, we move on. The feedback
loop that every other design discipline relies on — build it, watch it fail, fix it —
is almost entirely absent from ours. We are perhaps the only profession that designs
for human occupation and then systematically avoids observing the humans occupying it.

I am not arguing against beauty. I am arguing that we have confused the photograph
with the building, and the building is the thing people actually have to live inside.
