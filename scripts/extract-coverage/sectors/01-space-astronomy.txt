I want to tell you about the moment I stopped trusting my own eyes.

Six years ago our team pointed a very ordinary telescope at a very ordinary patch
of sky. We were not looking for anything. We were calibrating. And in the middle of
that calibration frame there was a smudge that had no business being there.

Now, smudges are common. Cosmic rays leave streaks. Dust on the sensor leaves
shadows. Satellites leave those long ugly scratches that every astronomer curses at
least once a week. So we did what you do: we threw the frame away and took another
one. The smudge was still there. We took a hundred more. It moved.

Anything that moves against the background stars is close. Close, in this business,
means somewhere inside our own solar system. And this thing was moving fast enough
that within about forty minutes we could sketch a rough trajectory. It was not
orbiting the sun the way asteroids do. It was falling through, on a hyperbolic path,
which means it came from outside and it will leave and it will never come back.

Let me put that in perspective. Every rock you have ever heard of — every asteroid,
every comet, every meteorite in every museum — formed here, in this system, out of
the same disk of debris that made the Earth under your feet. This one did not. This
one condensed around a different star, drifted through interstellar space for a span
of time we can only estimate in the hundreds of millions of years, and passed through
our neighbourhood at sixty thousand kilometres an hour while three graduate students
argued about whether the telescope needed cleaning.

We had eleven weeks. That is the whole observing window. After that it was too faint
for any instrument on Earth, and there is no spacecraft you can build and launch in
eleven weeks. So the question became: what can you learn about an object you can
barely see, that you will never see again?

More than you would think. Its brightness varied by a factor of ten as it tumbled,
which tells you it is elongated — cigar-shaped, or perhaps flattened like a pancake.
Its colour was reddish, consistent with a surface baked by cosmic radiation over
geological timescales. It showed no coma, no tail, none of the outgassing that makes
comets spectacular, and yet its trajectory deviated slightly from pure gravity, which
is the signature of something venting. A comet that isn't behaving like a comet.

I am not going to stand here and tell you it was a spacecraft. I do not believe that,
and the evidence does not require it. What I will tell you is that we were unprepared.
We had no protocol, no dedicated instrument, no rapid-response capability. We were
lucky, and we were slow, and we got a blurry photograph of the first confirmed visitor
from another star.

There will be others. The statistics say several pass through every year and we simply
miss them. The next survey telescope will find them routinely. And the difference
between a smudge we discard and a discovery we chase is not the telescope. It is
whether we decided, in advance, to be paying attention.
