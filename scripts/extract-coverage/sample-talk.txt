Chris Anderson: Let's start with the thing everybody gets wrong.

Dr. Elena Vasquez: Right. When people hear "carbon removal," they picture a machine
that sucks pollution out of the sky, and they assume it's science fiction. It isn't.
We've been running one for eleven months. But here's what nobody tells you — the
hard part was never the chemistry. [Laughter] The hard part is that the thing has to
run on electricity that's cleaner than the carbon it captures, or you've accomplished
absolutely nothing.

So let me back up. Roughly 37 billion tons of carbon dioxide enter the atmosphere
every year. If you wanted to remove even one percent of that, you'd need an industry
larger than global aviation, built from scratch, in under a decade. That's the scale
we're talking about. It's daunting, and I don't want to pretend otherwise.

But daunting isn't impossible, and I want to show you three things that changed my
mind. The first is thermodynamic. Pulling CO2 out of ambient air costs energy because
the gas is dilute — about 420 ppm, which sounds like nothing until you realize it's
enough to reshape the climate. Our first-generation sorbents needed 2,000 kilowatt-hours
per ton. The newest ones need under 900. That wasn't a breakthrough; it was eleven
thousand incremental experiments, most of which failed.

The second thing is geological. We inject the captured gas into basalt formations,
where it mineralizes — it literally turns to stone — in under two years. We didn't
expect that. The original models predicted centuries. When the first cores came back
from Iceland, our lead geochemist thought the samples had been mislabeled.

Chris Anderson: And the third?

Dr. Elena Vasquez: The third is economic, and it's the one that keeps me up at night.
For a long time, nobody would pay for this. There's no product. You're selling the
absence of something. But over the past four years, a handful of buyers started
purchasing removal as a commodity, and that created a price signal where none existed.
Once there's a price, engineers optimize against it. That's not idealism; that's just
how industries get built.

Now, I'd be doing you a disservice if I stopped there, because there are two objections
that I think are completely legitimate. The first is moral hazard. If people believe we
can clean up afterward, won't they emit more? Honestly, I don't know. The evidence is
genuinely mixed, and anyone who tells you they're certain is overselling their data.

The second objection is opportunity cost. Every dollar spent on removal is a dollar not
spent on prevention, and prevention is cheaper by an order of magnitude. That one I can
answer: we need both, and not sequentially. The emissions we can't eliminate — cement,
aviation, agriculture — will still be there in 2050. Somebody has to deal with them.

What I've learned, after eleven years of this, is that the interesting problems are
never the ones you expected. I trained as a chemist. I spend most of my time now on
permitting, supply chains, and convincing regulators that mineralized carbon isn't a
waste product. It's unglamorous. It's also the whole job.

Let me leave you with one number. If everything we're building works — if the sorbents
keep improving, if the basalt behaves, if the buyers keep buying — we'll remove maybe
a tenth of a percent of annual emissions by 2035. A tenth of a percent. That's not a
solution. It's a beginning, and beginnings are what we've got.

Thank you.
